Kicking off this week’s coverage, we turn our attention to Amazon’s latest strategic move that’s generating plenty of buzz in the retail tech arena. Amazon is now selling its AI-powered shopping tools to other retailers—a significant shift that reflects both the company’s ambition and the growing demand for artificial intelligence in ecommerce. Traditionally, Amazon has kept its advanced shopping technologies proprietary, leveraging AI to power product recommendations, personalized search, and even voice-enabled shopping through Alexa. But now, rather than limiting these sophisticated tools to its own ecosystem, Amazon is opening the doors for third-party retailers to license and integrate its AI shopping assistant technology.

This move signals Amazon’s desire to become not just a retail giant but also a key player in the broader B2B retail technology market. By allowing other retailers to access its AI expertise, Amazon stands to create a new revenue stream while influencing how competitors interact with their own customers. This could fundamentally level the playing field for smaller and mid-sized brands who previously couldn’t match Amazon’s AI-driven user experience. Retailers can now tap into advanced features like real-time product suggestions, automated customer support, and predictive inventory management without investing years in developing proprietary systems.

The implications are profound. On one hand, this could accelerate the adoption of AI across the retail sector, improving efficiency and customer satisfaction industry-wide. On the other, it raises questions about data privacy, platform dependency, and the potential for Amazon to gain even more insight into competitor performance and consumer preferences. For luxury brands, in particular, this could provide an avenue for delivering highly personalized experiences without the technical overhead, but it also means ceding some control to a major competitor. In the end, Amazon’s decision to sell its AI shopping tools marks a pivotal moment—not just for its own business model, but for the entire future of intelligent commerce.

Staying in the realm of ecommerce infrastructure, let’s shift gears and talk about Stord, a rising competitor in the fulfillment space that’s making headlines with a massive funding round. Stord has just raised $250 million at a staggering $3 billion valuation, positioning itself as a formidable alternative to Amazon’s own fulfillment network. What makes Stord distinctive is its approach to providing brands with a distributed network of warehouses, coupled with sophisticated inventory management software. This infrastructure allows brands—especially those operating online—to fulfill orders quickly and efficiently while maintaining direct ownership of their customer relationships.

For years, Amazon’s Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA) service has dominated the landscape, but it often comes at the cost of brand autonomy. By contrast, Stord’s value proposition is all about giving brands more control. They can leverage Stord’s technology to optimize inventory, route shipments intelligently, and access a nationwide—or even global—network without being locked into Amazon’s ecosystem. This is particularly appealing for premium and emerging brands who want fast, reliable logistics but are wary of handing over their customer data and experience.

The $250 million funding round is a clear vote of confidence from investors in both Stord’s business model and the broader trend of retailers seeking alternatives to Amazon. As ecommerce continues to boom, fulfillment remains a critical battleground. The ability to deliver products quickly and cost-effectively, while owning the end-to-end customer journey, is becoming a key differentiator for brands trying to build loyalty and stand out in a crowded marketplace. For luxury and jewellery brands, the stakes are even higher—customers expect impeccable service, and any misstep in fulfillment can erode brand equity. Stord’s rise reflects the growing recognition that logistics is no longer just a back-end function, but a core component of the customer experience. As more brands seek agility and independence, expect to see further innovation and competition in this crucial sector.

Speaking of brands seeking new platforms, Walmart is making waves as it evolves from a value-focused retailer to a launchpad for emerging and premium brands. Long associated with affordability and mass-market appeal, Walmart is now actively courting online-native and upscale brands looking to expand their reach into physical retail. This shift is transforming Walmart’s identity, making it a more attractive partner for brands that previously may have overlooked the retailer due to concerns about positioning or exclusivity.

The strategy is twofold: first, Walmart recognizes the changing preferences of its customers, many of whom are increasingly interested in discovering unique, high-quality products. Second, the retailer is tapping into the explosive growth of direct-to-consumer and digitally native brands that are seeking new channels for expansion. By providing access to its vast store network and robust ecommerce platform, Walmart enables these brands to scale rapidly across the U.S., reaching millions of potential new customers.

For emerging brands, this partnership offers a chance to test brick-and-mortar retail without the risk and expense of opening standalone stores. It also allows them to benefit from Walmart’s logistics, marketing, and customer service infrastructure. For Walmart, the arrival of premium and niche products helps the retailer broaden its appeal, attract new demographics, and foster an image of innovation and diversity. The move is not without challenges, though—balancing the needs and expectations of both value-seeking and premium shoppers is a delicate task. Yet, Walmart’s evolution underscores a broader trend: the lines between mass-market and luxury are blurring, and nimble retailers are finding creative ways to bridge the gap. As the market continues to fragment, expect more collaborations that redefine what it means to shop at America’s largest retailers.

On the topic of luxury and jewellery, Richemont—one of the world’s leading luxury conglomerates—recently reported robust sales growth in its jewellery brands over the past financial year. This comes even as revenue from its watch brands experienced a decline, reflecting shifting consumer preferences within the high-end segment. Despite swirling speculation about potential brand divestitures, Richemont’s leadership has firmly dismissed any plans to sell off its jewellery or watch labels, underscoring the company’s confidence in its long-term strategy.

Richemont’s jewellery portfolio, which includes iconic names like Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels, has continued to outperform, buoyed by resilient demand among affluent consumers. Jewellery, unlike watches, tends to be less cyclical and more closely tied to personal milestones, gifting, and self-expression. This dynamic has helped Richemont weather economic uncertainties and maintain strong margins. The company’s ability to innovate in design, storytelling, and customer experience has also contributed to its ongoing success.

The decline in watch sales, while notable, is part of a broader recalibration in the luxury sector. Younger consumers are gravitating toward jewellery as a form of investment and self-identity, while the pre-owned watch market has become more competitive. Richemont’s decision to downplay sale rumors is a signal to investors, partners, and clients that it remains committed to its core brands and sees ongoing opportunities for growth. For the luxury industry at large, Richemont’s performance is both a barometer of high-net-worth consumer sentiment and a lesson in portfolio management. As macroeconomic pressures continue to shape spending habits, brands that can pivot and double down on their strengths are well-positioned for the future.

Staying with the world of luxury but shifting to a more chaotic scene, the recent global launch of the Swatch x Audemars Piguet collection has generated headlines for all the wrong reasons. Intended as a celebration of collaboration between two iconic Swiss brands—Swatch, known for its accessible, playful watches, and Audemars Piguet, a byword for horological excellence—the launch was marked by large crowds, disorderly scenes, and even police intervention at retail locations worldwide. The chaos surrounding the event highlighted both the fervor of watch enthusiasts and the unintended consequences of hype-driven releases.

At the heart of the mayhem was the limited-edition nature of the collection, which fueled intense demand and, at times, frenzied behavior among shoppers. In major cities, long lines formed hours before stores opened, with some incidents escalating into physical altercations and intervention by law enforcement. While exclusivity and excitement are often hallmarks of luxury launches, the scale of disruption in this case raised concerns about safety, crowd management, and the reputational risks for the brands involved.

The incident is a stark reminder of the double-edged sword that is scarcity marketing. While limited releases can drive buzz and desirability, they must be managed carefully to avoid backlash and negative publicity. For both Swatch and Audemars Piguet, the challenge now is to balance consumer excitement with responsible retail practices—a lesson that resonates across the luxury industry. As brands continue to experiment with collaborations and exclusive drops, they must consider not only the potential for headline-grabbing sales but also the broader impact on customer trust and brand integrity. The chaos of this launch may serve as a cautionary tale for others planning high-profile releases.

Now, let’s turn to the technology sector and examine a trend with far-reaching implications for both ecommerce and luxury: the rising cost of smartphones and its impact on consumer demand, particularly in emerging markets like India. According to a recent report, rising prices—especially for entry-level smartphones—are poised to significantly dampen festive season demand, potentially reducing annual sales by as much as 30 percent. This is a sobering projection for brands that have long relied on India’s vast and growing population of smartphone users to drive growth.

The driving force behind this trend is a combination of inflation, increased component costs, and shifting currency dynamics, which have made it more expensive for brands to manufacture and sell affordable devices. For many consumers, the result is a choice between delaying their purchase or opting for a used or refurbished phone rather than a new one. This is particularly concerning during the festive period, a traditionally strong season for electronics sales.

For smartphone brands, the message is clear: affordability and value are becoming more important than ever. Companies that can offer innovative features at a reasonable price point stand to win market share, while those that focus solely on premium devices may find themselves squeezed. The implications extend to the broader ecommerce ecosystem as well—fewer new devices in circulation can impact mobile commerce growth, app adoption, and digital payment penetration. For brands operating in luxury and ecommerce, understanding these shifts is crucial for adapting marketing strategies and anticipating future demand. The Indian market, long seen as a growth engine, is entering a period of recalibration, and brands will need to innovate to stay ahead.

From smartphones to fashion, the theme of constrained consumer spending continues with weak demand at major apparel retailers Gap and American Eagle. Both companies have reported disappointing annual forecasts, reflecting a broader trend of shoppers pulling back on discretionary purchases amid ongoing economic uncertainty. This is particularly notable in the context of rising inflation, higher interest rates, and persistent cost-of-living pressures, all of which are weighing on household budgets and reshaping consumption patterns.

For Gap and American Eagle, the challenge is twofold: not only are consumers spending less overall, but they are also becoming more selective about where and how they shop. Value, quality, and brand relevance are top of mind, and retailers are having to work harder to earn a share of wallet. The apparel sector, already highly competitive, is seeing accelerated shifts toward discounting, promotional activity, and a renewed focus on core product lines. For luxury and premium brands, the message is clear—while aspirational shoppers remain important, resilience will depend on cultivating deeper engagement, loyalty, and a differentiated value proposition.

This constrained spending environment also has ripple effects across the supply chain, from raw materials to logistics and retail operations. Brands must find ways to optimize costs without sacrificing quality, invest in digital channels, and experiment with new models such as resale and rental. The weak demand at Gap and American Eagle is a wake-up call for the wider retail industry: the era of easy growth is over, and adaptability will be the key to thriving in a more cautious, value-driven marketplace.

As we close out our coverage, let’s pivot back to the world of artificial intelligence and hardware. After Nvidia’s blockbuster $20 billion not-acqui-hire—a term referring to acquiring talent rather than the company itself—AI chip startup Groq is reportedly seeking to raise $650 million in internal funding. This comes as Groq shifts its focus from hardware development to enhancing its AI inference capabilities, marking a significant strategic evolution for the company.

Groq’s original mission centered around building custom chips optimized for AI workloads, aiming to compete with giants like Nvidia and AMD. However, the landscape has shifted rapidly, with software and inference capabilities now seen as the true differentiators in the AI arms race. By doubling down on inference—the process of running trained AI models quickly and efficiently—Groq hopes to carve out a niche in a crowded market where speed, scalability, and cost-efficiency are paramount.

The rumored $650 million fundraising effort reflects both the intense investor interest in AI infrastructure and the high stakes involved. As demand for generative AI and real-time analytics continues to surge, companies across ecommerce, luxury, and beyond are seeking ever-faster, more efficient ways to deploy AI at scale. For luxury brands experimenting with personalization, predictive analytics, and immersive experiences, advances in AI hardware and inference could unlock new possibilities for engaging customers and optimizing operations. Groq’s pivot is emblematic of the broader shift in AI—from a focus on raw processing power to the nuanced challenge of delivering actionable insights in real time. As competition intensifies, expect to see more startups and established players alike recalibrating their strategies to stay ahead in this fast-moving field.
